1. Credit Report Review
Before You Start
Have your Social Security number and current address information available.
Use the official AnnualCreditReport.com site rather than unfamiliar credit-report websites.
Set aside enough time to review all three major credit reports: Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion.
Have a secure place to record discrepancies you find.
Step-by-Step Guide
Request your credit reports from the three major bureaus.
Review your personal information for incorrect names, addresses, or employers.
Check every credit account and confirm that the account belongs to you.
Look for incorrect balances, payment histories, account statuses, or late payments.
Review hard inquiries and identify any you do not recognize.
Dispute inaccurate information with the appropriate credit bureau and keep copies of your documentation.
Cost Estimate

DIY: $0
Official credit reports can be obtained free through AnnualCreditReport.com.

Professional: Usually $0–$150+
Credit-monitoring or identity-protection services may charge recurring fees, but paying for a service isn't required to review your reports.

2. Bicycle Tune-Up
Before You Start
Work in a well-lit area where the bicycle can be supported securely.
Gather a bicycle pump, tire-pressure gauge, hex keys, screwdrivers, bicycle lubricant, and clean rags.
Check the tire sidewalls for the manufacturer's recommended pressure.
If the bike has hydraulic brakes, damaged carbon components, or significant drivetrain problems, consider professional service.
Step-by-Step Guide
Check both tires for cuts, excessive wear, embedded debris, and proper pressure.
Test the front and rear brakes and make sure the levers feel firm.
Inspect the chain for rust, excessive dirt, or dry links; clean and lubricate it as needed.
Shift through the gears and check that the chain moves smoothly between gears.
Check that the wheels, handlebars, seat, and pedals are secure.
Take a short test ride and listen for unusual noises or shifting/braking problems.
Cost Estimate

DIY: $5–$30
Typical supplies include chain lubricant, cleaning supplies, and possibly an inner tube.

Professional: $75–$200+
A basic tune-up at a bicycle shop varies by location and the amount of adjustment required.

4. Check for Toilet Leaks
Before You Start
Have food coloring or leak-detection dye and paper towels available.
Make sure nobody needs to use the toilet during the test.
Locate the toilet's shutoff valve in case a leak requires immediate isolation.
Do not disassemble the toilet until you've identified the likely source of the leak.
Step-by-Step Guide
Remove the tank lid and inspect the water level and visible components.
Add about 10 drops of food coloring to the tank.
Do not flush the toilet for approximately 30 minutes.
Check the bowl for colored water.
If colored water appears, the flapper or flush valve is likely allowing water to escape.
Check around the base, supply connection, and tank bolts for external leaks.
Replace worn components or call a plumber if the source cannot be identified.
Cost Estimate

DIY: $0–$20
Food coloring is inexpensive; replacement flappers are commonly inexpensive.

Professional: Approximately $100–$300+
Cost depends on the failed component and whether the toilet needs more extensive repair.
