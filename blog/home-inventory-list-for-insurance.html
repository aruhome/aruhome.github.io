Home Inventory List for Insurance: What to Record Before You Need It

November 7, 2026 • 7 min read

After a fire, a break-in, or a burst pipe, the hard part is often remembering what you owned. A home inventory list is the record you wish you had made on an ordinary Saturday: the item, where it lived, what it cost, and a photo that proves it was yours.

Your policy decides what is covered and what you still have to document. This list is the paperwork people are usually asked to produce. It is not a promise that every item will be paid for.

What to write down for each item

Skip the junk drawer. Record the things that would be expensive or slow to replace.

  • What it is and which room it is in.
  • Brand, model, and serial number. The plate is usually on the back, inside a door, or on the frame of a major appliance.
  • Purchase date and price, or your best estimate if the receipt is gone.
  • A photo of the item and a close photo of the label.
  • The receipt or invoice, if you still have it. A card statement is a weak substitute, but it is better than nothing.
  • Warranty end date, when the item still has one. That is a separate problem from insurance, and it is worth keeping in the same record.

If you are photographing labels by hand, the serial-number scanning guide shows a faster way to capture the plate without typing it.

Walk the house in this order

One room at a time beats a single overwhelming list. Start with the systems and appliances, then the belongings that would actually hurt to replace.

  • Kitchen: refrigerator, range, dishwasher, microwave, disposal, and any small appliances you would rebuy.
  • Laundry: washer, dryer, and the vent. Note the model before a repair visit, not during one.
  • Utility area: water heater, furnace or air handler, sump pump, water softener, and the electrical panel location.
  • Living areas: television, audio equipment, desk computer, and furniture you paid real money for.
  • Bedrooms: mattresses, dressers, and electronics. Photograph jewelry and collections in place, then store the photos with the rest of the list.
  • Garage and outdoors: lawn equipment, tools, bicycles, and the grill. These are easy to forget because they are not in the main rooms.

Renters can use the same walk-through for belongings they own. The building’s furnace is the landlord’s asset. Your couch, laptop, and bicycle are yours. The renter inventory checklist is the shorter version of this list.

What is worth the extra minute

Spend the time on items with a serial number, a high replacement cost, or both. A set of everyday dishes does not need a line each. A camera, a bicycle, a musical instrument, and a refrigerator do.

For collections, one group photo plus a count is usually enough to start. Add individual photos for anything you would argue about later.

Also note improvements that are part of the house: a new roof, a replaced water heater, a renovated bathroom. Those are not “contents,” but they matter when you are talking to an insurer or a buyer. Keep the contractor invoice with the item.

Keep a copy you can open after the phone is gone

A list that lives only in one app, with no export, is a bad list for a disaster. Export a PDF and keep a second copy somewhere that is not the same room as the house. On iPhone, that can be an iCloud Drive backup you control. On either phone, a PDF you emailed to yourself or saved to a drive you already use is enough.

Update the list when you buy something expensive, not once a year in theory. A new appliance is a two-minute addition if the receipt is still in your hand.

A short version you can finish this weekend

  1. Photograph every major appliance label.
  2. Add brand, model, serial, room, and a rough purchase year.
  3. Attach the receipt if you can find it in ten minutes. Do not spend the afternoon hunting.
  4. Photograph electronics, tools, bikes, and anything over a few hundred dollars.
  5. Export one PDF and put a copy outside the house.

That is a usable inventory. You can fill in prices and older receipts later.
